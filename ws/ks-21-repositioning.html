体位たいい変換へんかん：床とこずれを ふせぐ

Repositioning — Turning on Schedule to Prevent Pressure Sores — Scenario Dialogue

Can-do: Turn a resident who cannot turn herself: say what you are about to do before you touch her, move her from her back onto her side, pack the new position with a pillow and a cushion, then check the skin you just uncovered.

1 · Dialogue & Why — 会話

Hana (caregiver)
田中たなかさん、体位たいいを 変かえましょうか。
Tanaka-san, shall we change your position?
Why: Ask, even when she cannot turn herself. A body that cannot move is still her body, and the question is what makes the next two minutes something you do together.
Tanaka-san (resident)
ええ、お願ねがいします。
Yes, please.
Why: Wait for this. If it does not come, ask again — never turn someone who has not answered you.
Hana (caregiver)
同おなじ 姿勢しせいが 長ながいと 床とこずれに なります。
Staying in one position too long causes pressure sores.
Why: THE RULE OF THIS EPISODE. Skin pressed between bone and mattress loses its blood supply in roughly two hours. Say the reason out loud — a resident who knows why the turning matters stops treating it as an interruption.
Tanaka-san (resident)
今いまは 仰向あおむけですね。
I'm on my back at the moment.
Why: She names the position she is in. Use her word back to her so you both mean the same thing.
Hana (caregiver)
はい。横向よこむきに なりましょうか。
Yes. Shall we turn you onto your side?
Why: Name the position you are going to, not just the fact of moving. 横向[よこむ]き tells her which way to help you, and it is the word the care record uses.
Tanaka-san (resident)
背中せなかが 少すこし 痛いたいです。
My back hurts a little.
Why: Pain in the part that has been under her weight is exactly what you are turning her for. Do not brush it away — it tells you where to look in a moment.
Hana (caregiver)
ゆっくりで 大丈夫だいじょうぶですよ。
There's no rush.
Why: Ep 19's sentence, doing the same work here. A person who expects to be moved quickly tenses, and a tense body is heavier and easier to hurt.
Hana (caregiver)
動うごかす まえに、かならず 声こえを かけます。
I always speak to you before I move you.
Why: An unannounced turn is the one that frightens people. 声[こえ]を かける — literally to put a voice on someone — is the standard phrase in every Japanese facility for this, and it is one short sentence before your hands do anything.
Hana (caregiver)
枕まくらと クッションを 入いれますね。
I'll tuck a pillow and a cushion in.
Why: The turn is only half the job. Without packing, she rolls flat again within minutes and the same skin takes the weight — the cushion is what makes the new position last.
Tanaka-san (resident)
これで 気持きもちが いいです。
That feels good now.
Why: Your confirmation that the packing sits right. If it does not come, take the cushion out and place it again rather than leaving her to settle.
Hana (caregiver)
背中せなかの 皮膚ひふを 確認かくにんしますね。
I'll check the skin on your back.
Why: The moment after the turn is the only time that skin is visible. Look at it every single turn — this is the check that catches a sore while it is still just a red patch.
Hana (caregiver)
赤あかい ところが あったら、すぐ 報告ほうこくします。
If there's a red patch, I report it straight away.
Why: Redness that does not fade is stage one of a pressure sore, and it is a nurse's call, not yours. Report it the same shift — by tomorrow the skin may already be broken.
Tanaka-san (resident)
ありがとう。安心あんしんしました。
Thank you. That's a relief.
Why: 安心[あんしん]しました is what you are actually working for. Someone who cannot move herself is trusting you to come back — say when you will.
Hana (caregiver)
また あとで 向むきを 変かえに 来きますね。
I'll come back later to turn you again.
Why: Repositioning is on a schedule, not on request — she cannot call you by turning over. Saying you will return is how she knows the next turn is already yours to remember.
