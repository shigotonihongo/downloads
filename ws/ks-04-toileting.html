排泄はいせつの 介助かいじょ

Toileting Assistance — Helping with Dignity — Scenario Dialogue

Can-do: Invite a resident to the toilet discreetly, assist while protecting their privacy and dignity, and confirm they feel better.

1 · Dialogue & Why — 会話

Hana (caregiver)
田中たなかさん、そろそろ お手洗てあらいに 行いきませんか。
Tanaka-san, shall we head to the toilet soon?
Why: お手洗[てあら]い ('hand-washing place') is the polite, softer word for toilet — gentler than トイレ. そろそろ ('about time now') makes a timed, low-key suggestion, and 行[い]きませんか ('won't you go?') is a soft invitation. Say this quietly and privately — never announce toileting across a room.
Tanaka-san (resident)
うん…、実じつは さっきから 我慢がまんしていたの。
Mm… actually I've been holding it for a while.
Why: Residents often hold on (我慢[がまん]する) out of embarrassment rather than ask. That's exactly why we offer on a gentle schedule instead of waiting to be asked — it prevents accidents (失禁[しっきん]) and protects pride. 実[じつ]は = 'actually / to tell the truth', softening an admission.
Hana (caregiver)
そうでしたか。すぐ ご案内あんないしますね。恥はずかしくないですよ。
I see. I'll take you right away. There's nothing to be embarrassed about.
Why: ご案内[あんない]します is the humble form of 案内[あんない]する ('to guide') — ご + Sino-Japanese noun + します. Naming the feeling and easing it ('恥[は]ずかしくないですよ' — nothing to be embarrassed about) directly protects the resident's dignity, which is the heart of 排泄介助[はいせつかいじょ].
Hana (caregiver)
ズボンを 下おろしますね。手てすりに つかまってください。
I'll lower your trousers, okay. Please hold on to the handrail.
Why: ALWAYS announce an action before you touch or move clothing — 〜しますね tells the resident what's about to happen so they're never startled or exposed without warning. 手[て]すりに つかまってください ('hold the handrail') keeps them steady: falls are a real risk in the bathroom.
Hana (caregiver)
終おわったら 呼よんでくださいね。外そとで 待まっています。
Call me when you're finished. I'll be waiting outside.
Why: This one line is pure dignity. Where it's safe, you step out of sight and let the resident have privacy (プライバシー) — standing and watching strips away their pride. 終[お]わったら = 'when you're finished'; 呼[よ]んでください = 'please call me'. You stay close, but out of view.
Tanaka-san (resident)
ありがとう。もう 大丈夫だいじょうぶよ。
Thank you. I'm done now.
Why: もう = 'already / now'; もう大丈夫[だいじょうぶ] = 'I'm okay now / all done'. When you hear this, respond calmly and help them finish — no reaction to smell or mess, ever. Staying matter-of-fact is itself a form of respect.
Hana (caregiver)
きれいに しますね。手てを 洗あらいましょう。
I'll clean you up. Let's wash your hands.
Why: きれいにします ('I'll make you clean') is a gentle way to say you'll wipe/tidy — softer than the blunt 拭[ふ]く. Then 手[て]を洗[あら]いましょう ('let's wash hands') — hygiene matters, and phrasing it as 'let's' keeps it a shared, dignified routine rather than a command.
Hana (caregiver)
すっきりしましたね。よく 我慢がまんされましたね。
You feel better now, don't you. You held on so well.
Why: A dignifying close. すっきりしましたね shares the relief, and 我慢[がまん]されました uses 尊敬語[そんけいご] (される, the honorific of する) to gently praise the resident — turning a moment that could feel shameful into one where they're respected. Small words like this build trust (信頼関係[しんらいかんけい]).
